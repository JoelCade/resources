Power Platform Disaster Recovery Principles

What the platform recovers for you, what stays your responsibility, and how to build a business continuity plan that survives contact with a real outage · September 2026.

The one thing to remember: Power Platform gives you very strong in-region resilience for free and automatically, but everything beyond that is a decision you have to make. Cross-region protection is opt-in (self-service disaster recovery), backups expire in days not years, and none of it covers the systems your apps and flows actually talk to. Disaster recovery on Power Platform is therefore mostly an exercise in scoping what the platform does not cover, and writing a plan for that part.

1. Start with the shared responsibility line

Almost every failed DR conversation starts in the same place: someone assumes "it is SaaS, Microsoft handles it." That is half true, and the half that is not true is where the outage hurts. Draw the line explicitly before you design anything.

Microsoft

What the platform handles

  • Synchronous replication of production environments across at least two availability zones in the region.
  • Automatic detection of and failover from zone-level failures, with no admin action required.
  • Continuous automated system backups of every environment that has a Dataverse database.
  • Physical, power, cooling and network redundancy, and data residency within the geography.
You

What stays yours

  • Deciding whether an environment needs cross-region protection, and turning it on.
  • Deciding when to fail over. Microsoft does not pull the trigger for cross-region failover.
  • Recovery of everything outside Dataverse: connectors, external systems, gateways, integrations.
  • Long-term retention and archival. Platform backups are a rollback tool, not an archive.
  • Testing. A plan that has never been rehearsed is a document, not a capability.

2. Fix the vocabulary before the architecture

DR discussions collapse when the business and the platform team use the same words differently. Agree these three definitions per workload, in writing, before choosing any technology.

TermThe question it answersHow to express it usefully
RPO
Recovery point objective		How much data are we willing to lose?In minutes or hours of transactions, not as "zero." Genuine zero is rarely affordable and almost never required.
RTO
Recovery time objective		How long can the business run without this?Measured from the moment of impact to the moment users are productive again, including the time it takes a human to decide to act.
RLO
Recovery level objective		What does "recovered" actually mean?Which processes must work on day one. Order intake may be critical while reporting can wait a week. This is what keeps a DR plan affordable.
The number everyone forgets: decision time. Cross-region failover on Power Platform is customer-initiated. If your incident process takes four hours to reach a person with authority to press the button, your real RTO is four hours plus the failover, no matter what the platform is capable of. Pre-authorize the decision, name the decision maker, and define the trigger conditions in advance.

3. The three layers of resilience

Power Platform resilience is not one feature. It is three distinct mechanisms that protect against three different kinds of failure. Confusing them is the most common architectural error in this space.

Layer 1 — Availability zones (you already have this)

Every production environment is replicated synchronously across at least two, and often three, availability zones within its region. Zones sit in physically separate data centers with independent power, cooling and networking, typically several kilometers apart and generally within 100 kilometers. Zone failures are detected and routed around automatically, with near-zero data loss and recovery measured in single-digit minutes.

Two consequences worth stating plainly to stakeholders. First, this is why cross-region secondary copies stopped being maintained by default: zone redundancy made them redundant, and recovering from them was slow and manual. Second, this protection applies to production environments only. Business-critical data sitting in a sandbox, developer or trial environment does not get the same treatment, which is one of many reasons production workloads should never live there.

Layer 2 — Self-service disaster recovery (opt-in, per environment)

Self-service disaster recovery (SSDR) maintains an asynchronous copy of a production environment in the paired Azure region, typically at least 300 miles away, and gives you a button to fail over, fail back, and rehearse. It is the only mechanism that survives the loss of a whole region.

Read this twice: in the public cloud, the platform does not keep a copy of your environment in a remote region unless you turn SSDR on. Database backups are not replicated to a secondary region either. If your business continuity plan assumes a regional outage is survivable and SSDR is off, the plan is wrong.

Layer 3 — Backup and restore (the one you will actually use)

Regional disasters are rare. Bad deployments, runaway flows and accidental bulk deletes are not. In practice, restore is the recovery mechanism most organizations invoke, and it is the one most often found wanting because of retention limits nobody checked.

Practical constraints to design around:

  • 28 days is the ceiling. There is no native long-term retention. If a regulator expects seven years, that is an export and archive pattern you build yourself, not a backup setting you flip.
  • You cannot download a backup. Offline copies of a Dataverse backup are not supported, so backups are not an exit strategy or an air-gapped archive.
  • Extended retention needs a Managed Environment, which means a premium license tier for the users of that environment.
  • Restores are not instant. Large databases, particularly with audit data, can take more than a day. Put that number in your RTO, not an optimistic guess.
  • Restore covers solution-aware assets. Backup and restore includes apps and flows that live inside a Dataverse solution. Anything outside a solution is outside the safety net — which is a resilience argument for solid ALM discipline, not just a release-management one.
  • Take a manual backup before every significant change, such as a major deployment, a version update or a data migration. This is the cheapest insurance on the platform.

5. Seven principles that hold up under pressure

1

Tier your environments, do not protect everything equally

SSDR costs a premium license tier and a second copy of your storage. Applying it uniformly is how DR programs get cancelled. Classify workloads into tiers, and let the tier drive the mechanism.

2

Design for the failure you will actually have

Ranked by likelihood: a bad deployment, a bad bulk operation, a broken integration, an expired credential, a regional outage. Most DR effort is spent on the last one. Most incidents are the first four. Weight your investment accordingly.

3

Treat DR as an extension of ALM, not a separate discipline

If your solutions are in source control and you can rebuild an environment from a pipeline, you already have a recovery path that no backup retention window can expire. Configuration should be reproducible; only data should need restoring.

4

Map the blast radius beyond Dataverse

Failing over Dataverse does not fail over the SQL Server, ERP, REST API or third-party service your flows depend on. Those systems have their own RPO, and it governs the end-to-end result. Draw the dependency map first, then set an honest objective for the composite.

5

Pre-authorize the decision

Because failover is customer-initiated, name the person who can call it, define the thresholds that justify it, and give them a way to see current replication lag so the data-loss trade-off is a fact rather than a guess.

6

Rehearse, on a copy, on a schedule

An untested plan is a hypothesis. Drills are supported natively and involve no data loss, but they do involve downtime for the environment being failed over — so run them against a copy of production rather than production itself.

7

Write down the degraded-mode process

For every tier-one workload, answer: what does the business do during the recovery window? A paper form, a shared mailbox or a manual queue is a legitimate answer. Not having an answer is not.

6. What does not fail over cleanly

This is the section that separates a plan from a wish. A Dataverse failover moves the environment; it does not move your ecosystem. Validate each of these explicitly.

External systems and connectors

Connectors that depend on SQL Server, SharePoint, ERP or third-party services can have recovery problems of their own. Their availability and RPO are outside Power Platform's commitments entirely.

Hard-coded endpoints and allow lists

Anything pinned to a primary-region hostname or IP range breaks after failover. Firewall allow lists must include the secondary region's ranges too, or outbound traffic silently fails.

Analytics links

Fabric link and Synapse Link do not currently fail over with the environment and may need to be unlinked and recreated. Neither offers an RTO or RPO objective during failover today.

Event endpoints

Webhook, Service Bus and Event Grid registrations can stop dispatching after a failover or database refresh. Events continue to be generated but are not delivered until the endpoints are recreated and validated.

Power Automate throughput

Environments enabled for SSDR run on isolated runtime capacity. Most workloads see no difference, but high-volume, highly parallel or latency-sensitive flows may start slower or throttle. Validate business-critical automation before enabling.

Networking and identity plumbing

Environments deployed into a virtual network need a virtual network pair configured, or failover fails outright because cross-region connectivity cannot be established.

7. Turning on cross-region protection: the preconditions

  • Production only. SSDR is available for production environments. Sandbox and developer environments are not eligible.
  • Managed Environment required, which carries a premium license requirement for that environment's users.
  • Storage doubles. The secondary copy draws from your pooled tenant Dataverse entitlements. A 10 GB environment consumes another 10 GB when protected. The capacity graph shows the extra consumption with a disaster-recovery indicator.
  • Region pairing is a hard dependency. Regions without an Azure pair cannot offer SSDR, and failover is only ever to the designated pair — you cannot choose an arbitrary region. Confirm your geography's support status before you promise anything.
  • Enablement takes time. Provisioning and initial replication can take up to 48 hours. It does not disrupt the environment while it runs, but it is not a same-afternoon response to an unfolding incident.
  • Turning it off deletes the secondary copy, and it cannot be turned off while the environment is running from the secondary region.

9. Running a drill

  1. Copy production to a sandbox, then convert it to a production type. Drills involve real downtime for the environment being failed over, so do not rehearse against live production.
  2. Enable disaster recovery on the copy and allow replication to complete.
  3. Check replication lag first. The admin center shows the last sync time before you commit, which tells you the data-loss exposure at that moment. You can inspect it and cancel.
  4. Fail over, then validate the platform: user sign-in, security roles, application functionality, and the business-critical processes themselves.
  5. Validate the ecosystem: Azure integrations, external APIs, custom plug-ins and middleware, Power Automate flows, agents, Power Apps and Power Pages workloads, and every mission-critical third-party connector.
  6. Fail back, and time both directions. Compare against your stated RTO and correct the objective if reality disagrees with it.
  7. Record the outcome and the gaps. Microsoft does not publish a mandatory drill checklist, so your evidence is what auditors will read.
Know what a drill cannot prove: during a rehearsal the primary region stays healthy, so anything that quietly still resolves to the primary region will appear to work. Hard-coded primary endpoints are the classic example. Look for them deliberately rather than trusting a green drill.

PatternWhy it fails
"It is SaaS, so DR is covered"In-region resilience is automatic; cross-region is opt-in and everything outside Dataverse is yours. The assumption is discovered to be false during the incident.

Business-critical data in a sandbox or developer environmentNon-production environments do not receive the same resilience posture, and some cannot be restored the way admins expect.
Components living outside solutionsThey fall outside the restore path and outside any rebuild pipeline. Recovery becomes manual reconstruction from memory.

11. A short checklist

  • Every workload has a written RPO, RTO and RLO agreed with the business.
  • Environments are tiered, and the tier drives the protection mechanism.
  • Production data lives only in production environments.
  • Solutions are in source control with a tested rebuild path.
  • Manual backup is a standard step in the pre-deployment runbook.
  • Backup retention is set deliberately, not left at the default by accident.
  • Tier-one environments have SSDR enabled, with capacity and licensing accounted for.
  • The dependency map lists every external system and its own recovery objective.
  • Firewall allow lists and virtual network pairing cover the secondary region.
  • A named individual is pre-authorized to declare failover, against defined triggers.
  • A drill has been run in the last 12 months, and the findings were fixed.
  • Every tier-one process has a documented degraded-mode fallback.
