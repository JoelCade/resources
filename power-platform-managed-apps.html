Microsoft Copilot & Power Platform · Preview Capability Guide

Managed Apps (Preview)

Apps described in plain language in Copilot Cowork or Copilot Studio, generated as real full-stack code, published onto a Microsoft-operated runtime in your tenant, and governed from the Microsoft 365 admin center. How to build them, how they version toward production, how to govern them, and how the usage-based bill actually works. · September 2026.

The one thing to remember: a managed app is what you get when someone describes an app in Copilot Cowork or Copilot Studio and publishes it. It is a real full-stack application built with agentic coding, wired to your data through connectors, authenticated with Microsoft Entra, and governed from the Microsoft 365 admin center rather than the Power Platform admin center. Commercially it is a hybrid, and that is the part people get wrong: building is usage-based and paid in Copilot Credits, while the capability to run apps built in Cowork is included with Power Apps Premium — drawing on that license's allowance of 40,000 Power Platform requests per user per 24 hours. AI-powered runtime inside the app always consumes credits on top.

1. What a managed app actually is

App building starts as a conversation. A user chooses App (Preview) from the Copilot Studio home screen, or invokes the /app skill in a Copilot Cowork chat, and describes the business outcome, the users, the data, and the actions they need. Agentic coding turns that intent into a working app scaffold. From there the user iterates in natural language, previews the app, and can inspect the underlying code when they want more control.

What comes out the other side is deliberately not a toy:

The artifact

A full-stack app built on open standards

  • Real code, inspectable and extensible by developers who want to go deeper.
  • Git-backed source control, deployment stages, and version isolation, so the next version can be improved without disturbing the live one.
  • Published apps are discovered by users at managedapps.cloud.microsoft.com, or opened from a share link.
The platform around it

Enterprise controls, on by default

  • Microsoft Entra authentication and authorization with no extra identity configuration.
  • Access to more than 1,500 connectors, plus Work IQ for organizational context.
  • Conditional Access, DLP, advanced connector policies, sharing limits, and data source restrictions applied automatically.
  • Microsoft-managed environments that inherit those policies rather than requiring you to configure them.

Two front doors, one engine

The important structural fact: Copilot Studio is the engine behind both surfaces. Cowork is not a separate, lesser app builder that produces a different kind of artifact. The building experience, the resulting app, and the governance are the same, which means a user can start in one surface and continue in the other without relearning anything or migrating anything.

SurfaceHow you startAvailability

Apps, agents, and workflows are three different tools

Copilot Studio now hosts all three, and the reason to care is that people reach for the wrong one constantly. They are not competing options; they solve different shapes of problem, and a serious business process often needs more than one.

Structure

Apps

Guide a person through data, forms, lists, and actions in an interactive experience. Reach for an app when a human needs a place to go and work.

Reasoning

Agents

Answer questions and take action conversationally. Reach for an agent when the problem is interpretation and judgment rather than structured data entry.

Consistency

Workflows

Execute repeatable steps against defined triggers with defined outcomes. Reach for a workflow when the process must happen the same way every time, without a person.

In combination

Most real processes

An intake app, a workflow that routes and notifies, and an agent that answers questions about the backlog. Building all three in one studio is the actual argument for this capability.

Name collision worth getting straight before a customer conversation. "Managed apps" is the Microsoft 365 admin center's term for apps built this way. It does not mean Managed Environments, and it is not the same thing as a Power Apps code app or a Power Apps vibe app, even though all three involve generated code. The admin center draws the line explicitly, and the line is about where the app is governed.

2. Building one

1

Describe the outcome, not the screens

State the business problem, who uses it, what data it needs, and what actions it takes. You can reference Microsoft 365 sources directly — documents, spreadsheets, meetings, messages. "Build an app to run my daily scrum with my team" is a legitimate starting prompt; so is a paragraph describing an onboarding tracker with its data sources named.

Specificity about data and actions pays off more than specificity about layout. Layout is cheap to change in the next turn. A missing data source means the scaffold is built around the wrong shape.

In Copilot Studio you can also choose the model and the reasoning level before generating. Treat that as a real lever: a higher reasoning level on a genuinely complex first generation usually costs less than three corrective turns afterward.

2

Let it connect the data

Apps use connectors to reach business data, and Work IQ to reach organizational context inside the Microsoft 365 tenant boundary. This is not read-only: an app can surface information, take action, and write results back to the connected system. The app-building skill can also generate a data schema in supported connectors and hand you an app with those sources already wired.

1,500+ connectors Work IQ organizational context Schema generation Read and write
3

Iterate in conversation, inspect the code when you need to

Ask for changes in natural language — add a feature, change a layout, modify how data is displayed — and each change is applied and reflected in the preview without manually rebuilding the interface. Information workers can stay entirely in the conversation.

Advanced makers and developers can switch between the live app and a code view, or show both at once, to inspect the files created during generation. That matters for two reasons beyond curiosity: it is how a developer validates that the generated app does what it claims, and it is the path to extending the app when a scenario needs deeper control. An app nobody can read is not an enterprise asset.

4

Work from the app workspace once you have more than one

In Copilot Studio, Apps in the navigation opens a workspace listing the apps you have built. Search by name and review publishing status, owner, environment, and last modified date; open one to keep building, or use Play to test it as a user would. This becomes the maker's home surface quickly — the tile on the landing page is for starting, the workspace is for operating.

5

Publish, then share deliberately

Publishing makes the app and your latest changes available to the people you share it with. Sharing works like a Word document or a PowerPoint deck: generate a link and send it to anyone in your organization who can use Microsoft 365 Copilot, or add users directly.

Tell every builder this once, in these words: anyone with the share link can open and use the app including all of its data. A share link is a data-access decision, not a convenience. Share only with the people who should see what the app can see.

What a good first prompt looks like

The difference between a usable first generation and a frustrating one is almost entirely in how much structure the prompt carries. Compare a bare request with one that names the fields, the roles, the states, the views, and the notifications:

Create an app to manage equipment maintenance requests.

Include an intake form capturing: asset ID, fault category, priority,
reported-by, description, requested completion date, and photo attachments.

Assign each request to an owner, a reviewer, and an approver.

Use the statuses: New, Triaged, In progress, Blocked, Completed.

Add views for My requests, Awaiting my approval, and All open requests.

Notify the owner when a request is assigned and the requester when
the status changes.

Everything in that prompt is a decision the generator would otherwise make for you, and every one of those decisions is cheaper to state than to correct. Layout is the one thing worth leaving vague — it is trivial to adjust in the next turn.

3. Versioning and the path to production

This is the section that separates a managed app from a disposable prototype, and it is the part most easily missed in a demo.

Apps follow familiar application lifecycle management principles. The app's human-readable source can be versioned through Git-backed source control. Makers can preview and test changes before publishing. Crucially, there is version isolation: the published version stays stable in production while the next version is being built, so iteration does not put the live experience at risk.

Why this changes the conversation: the standard objection to AI-generated apps is "fine for a prototype, unsafe for anything real." Separate build, preview, and publish stages with a stable production version answer that directly. It is the difference between a clever demo and something you can put a business process on. When you evaluate this capability, evaluate this, not the speed of the first generation.

The Microsoft-operated runtime

A published app runs on a Microsoft-operated runtime inside your tenant. The platform hosts both the client and the service logic and applies organizational security and policy to it, rather than handing you an artifact you then have to host and secure yourself. What that runtime supplies:

  • Microsoft Entra identity for authentication and access control.
  • Conditional Access enforcement.
  • Advanced connector policies and data loss prevention.
  • Monitoring and inventory, so administrators can see what exists.
  • Usage analytics out of the box, without instrumenting the app yourself.

The practical consequence: there is no hosting decision, no certificate to manage, no identity integration to write, and no separate monitoring stack to stand up. That is most of the reason this is worth using instead of generating a web app with a coding agent and hosting it yourself.

Work IQ: organizational context, not just data access

Apps integrate natively with Work IQ, which contributes three distinct things worth separating:

CapabilityWhat it means in the app
ContextThe app understands the user and the organization around them — people, relationships, and how work connects.
DataRetrieval of work and business data that is permission-aware, so a user sees only what they are already entitled to see.
WorkspacesSecure retention of user inputs and app outputs, so the app has somewhere to keep state.

Permission-aware retrieval is the detail to raise with a security reviewer. It means the app does not become a way to see data the user could not otherwise reach.

4. What sits underneath

Managed apps run in Microsoft-managed environments, and enabling app creation changes Power Platform environment routing in your tenant whether or not you were using it before. This is the part administrators find out about late, so it is worth reading carefully.

  • Environment routing is turned on automatically for apps, in every tenant, and cannot be changed. It is a premium governance feature and apps enable it by default.
  • Makers are routed to their own personal developer environments, which are managed environments by default. In a tenant with no prior routing rules, a rule routing Everyone to personal developer environments is created automatically, along with a new default environment group.
  • Tenants with existing routing rules do not get that new rule, which means some makers may be unable to use apps until an admin adds a rule covering Everyone. If you already use routing, this is an action item, not a notification.
  • The Everyone rule must sit last in priority order, and only one rule may target Everyone. Existing configurations that violate either constraint must be cleaned up before any further routing changes can be made.
  • These personal developer environments have no Dataverse initially. Dataverse is added automatically only when the maker visits a Power Apps or Power Automate maker surface and lands in that environment, or an admin adds it deliberately.
The licensing tripwire hidden in that list: a maker who only builds managed apps in their personal developer environment does not need a Power Apps or Power Automate premium license, and auto-claim does not apply. The moment that maker creates a Power Apps or Power Automate resource in that environment, they do need the appropriate premium license to satisfy managed environment requirements, and auto-claim does apply. The boundary is the resource type, not the environment.

5. Governing them

Enablement

  1. Copilot Cowork access is granted by a spending policy, not by an allow list. Cowork is generally available, and the old practice of allowing or blocking users on the Cowork agent entry under Agents → All Agents no longer controls access. Access exists when a spending policy includes the user and selects Cowork as a service.
  2. Enabling or disabling Cowork enables or disables app building by default. They are not separate switches.
  3. App creation in Cowork additionally requires Frontier enrollment, which only a Global Administrator can complete. Power Platform administrators cannot enable it and are directed to contact someone who can. AI Administrator, AI Reader, and Global Reader are view-only for this.
A spending policy is an access control, not just a budget. Any user in scope of a policy that selects Cowork can use Cowork, regardless of how small the limit is. A policy with a one-credit limit still grants access. To keep someone out, leave them out of every Cowork-selecting policy rather than setting their limit low. Administrators who try to fence off access with tiny limits end up with access they did not intend to grant and a support queue they did not expect.

The inventory: Microsoft 365 admin center → Apps → All apps

Every published app appears here automatically, with no manual registration. Created, updated, and deleted apps show up within about 15 minutes.

WhereWhat you get
The tableName, owner, status, hosting location, environment group, and creation date by default. Additional columns include created by, last modified, app ID, and owner ID. Summary cards show total apps and blocked apps. Supports filter, sort, keyword search, column choice, CSV export, and refresh.

Two actions that matter

Reversible

Block

Immediately stops end-user access while preserving the app and its data. Users attempting to launch it see a blocked status. Use this for an app under investigation — flagged for non-compliance, suspicious behavior, or a security concern — where deletion would destroy evidence. Unblock once resolved.

Permanent

Delete

Permanently removes the app, with a confirmation dialog summarizing the effect. This is the housekeeping tool for abandoned, test, and duplicate apps — which, in a tenant where anyone can produce an app in a conversation, is a category that grows on its own.

6. Licensing and cost

There are two meters here, and they are funded differently. Building apps falls under usage-based billing and is paid in Copilot Credits, from the same pool and governance model as Cowork itself. Running apps built in Cowork is included with Power Apps Premium. Getting this wrong in either direction produces a badly wrong forecast, so separate the two before you model anything.

The Power Apps Premium entitlement, precisely

Microsoft's request-limits documentation states it plainly: the capability to run apps built in Cowork is included with Power Apps Premium. The Power Platform requests those apps generate count toward that license's standing allowance.

DetailWhat it means
The allowance 40,000 Power Platform requests per user per 24 hours — the standard Power Apps Premium limit, not a separate app-specific pool. It is a daily figure, which is roughly 1.2 million a month, so it is far more generous than a monthly reading of the same number would suggest.
What counts Power Platform requests: API requests to connectors and Dataverse generated as the app runs.
Licenses that do not include it Power Apps per app, Power Apps pay-as-you-go, and Dynamics 365 licenses that include Power Apps capabilities. This is the trap: holding a Power Apps license of some kind is not sufficient. It must be Premium.
Never included AI-powered runtime — agents and Work IQ APIs invoked by the app — sits outside these limits and consumes Copilot Credits regardless of which license the user holds.
Stacking A user with multiple paid licenses gets the sum of their allowances. Someone holding both a Dynamics 365 Enterprise base license and a Power Apps per user license has 80,000 requests per 24 hours.
Two ways people misread this. First, as "40,000 operations a month" — it is per user per day, roughly thirty times larger, and reading it as monthly makes the entitlement look far tighter than it is. Second, as "Power Apps Premium covers managed apps, so there is no credit cost" — it covers running apps built in Cowork, not building them, and it never covers AI runtime inside the app. The documented sentence names apps built in Cowork specifically; if your scenario is apps authored in Copilot Studio, confirm the current entitlement wording before you commit to it commercially.

What sits inside usage-based billing

The Microsoft 365 admin center currently manages these services under usage-based billing:

  • Cowork itself — model responses, tool and skill calls, image generation, browser tasks.
  • Apps built with Copilot Cowork — the building, and the running where it is not covered by a Power Apps Premium entitlement.
  • Work IQ API — including when an app calls it.

More services are added over time, and spending policies have Auto-apply new services enabled by default. If you want new services reviewed before they inherit an existing policy, turn that setting off deliberately. Otherwise a future service quietly joins a budget someone already approved.

How you pay

MechanismWhat it isWhere it is configured

Pay-as-you-goBilled against a connected Azure subscription as consumed.Copilot → Cost management → Configuration

The Cost management dashboard reports consumption by policy, user, group, agent, and service. Reader roles can review consumption without gaining permission to change policies or billing configuration, which means finance and licensing stakeholders can be given visibility without being given control. Use that separation rather than handing out administrator roles for reporting.

Preview behavior to warn users about: a user who does not meet the credit requirements to run an app first receives a warning. Access is then blocked after 20 app operations or five minutes of use, whichever comes first. If a pilot audience has not been placed in a funded spending policy, they will hit that wall mid-demo and conclude the app is broken.

The cost mistakes worth pre-empting

  • Assuming there is no per-user option. There is. Where users already hold Power Apps Premium, the run cost for Cowork-built apps is already covered, and the remaining variable is the build activity and any AI runtime. An organization with a large Premium estate is in a very different position from one starting cold.
  • Assuming a Power Apps license is a Power Apps license. Per app, pay-as-you-go, and Dynamics 365 licenses carrying Power Apps capabilities do not include this. Check the actual SKU before promising anything.
  • Reading the allowance as monthly. 40,000 requests per user per 24 hours. Getting this wrong understates the headroom by roughly thirty times and leads to buying capacity nobody needs.
  • Forgetting AI runtime is always metered. An app that calls an agent or the Work IQ APIs consumes Copilot Credits on every invocation, whatever license the user holds. An app that calls an agent on every page load is a consumption decision disguised as a design decision.
  • Using low limits as an access control. Covered above, and worth repeating because it is the single most common misconfiguration: inclusion in a Cowork-selecting policy is the grant.
  • Leaving auto-apply on without deciding to. It is on by default and it is the right setting for many organizations. It should still be a decision someone made.
  • Missing the premium-license boundary in personal developer environments. Building managed apps there does not require Power Apps or Power Automate premium. Creating Power Apps or Power Automate resources in that same environment does, with auto-claim in play.
  • Ignoring what sits behind the connectors. Neither credits nor a Power Apps license pays for the third-party SaaS, Azure services, or Dynamics 365 licensing that the connectors reach into.
  • Pricing a preview. Frontier previews run under existing preview terms, and capabilities and availability may change. Confirm against the Copilot Credits Guide and the Power Platform Licensing Guide before putting a figure in a proposal.

7. Power Apps or Copilot Studio?

This is the question that comes up in every conversation about managed apps, and the binary framing is the wrong one. Both are enterprise-ready. They differ in authoring experience and commercial model, not in whether they can be trusted with a business process. The right question is which one fits the problem in front of you today, on the understanding that you can combine them later.

Predictable and precise

Power Apps fits when…

  • The organization already owns and runs Power Apps, with the skills and governance in place.
  • Costs must be fully predictable and forecastable per user, not variable with usage.
  • The workload is tied to Dynamics 365 or deep Dataverse data modeling.
  • Design precision matters — drag-and-drop layout control and Power Fx for exact logic.
  • Governance is expected to live in the Power Platform admin center.
  • The app itself is the deliverable, with AI optional rather than central.
Fast and consumption-based

Copilot Studio fits when…

  • You want to widen who can build beyond the existing maker community.
  • The organization prefers usage-based billing and wants to start without licensing every potential user up front.
  • Microsoft 365 Copilot is already deployed and Copilot Credits are already being managed.
  • The process needs an app, an agent, and a workflow together.
  • Natural-language authoring with optional code inspection suits the people doing the building.
  • Governance is expected to live in the Microsoft 365 admin center.
The commercial distinction, restated: these are not two sealed pricing worlds. Power Apps is per-user licensing — higher certainty, paid whether or not the app is used heavily. Copilot Studio app building is consumption — no new SKU to get started and no need to license a potential audience in advance, but a bill that moves with usage. They overlap: Power Apps Premium already includes the capability to run apps built in Cowork, within its 40,000 requests per user per 24 hours. So an organization with an established Premium estate can adopt this with the run cost largely covered, leaving build activity and AI runtime as the variable. Which approach is cheaper depends on the licenses already in place and how concentrated the usage turns out to be — and the second of those is usually unknown until after the app exists.

Three things that are true regardless of which you start with:

  • Existing Power Apps investments carry forward. Apps keep running. Business logic, Dataverse tables, and connectors to systems of record keep doing their job and can be reused across apps, workflows, and agents. Choosing Copilot Studio for a new build does not strand anything already built.
  • An existing Premium estate is an asset here, not a sunk cost. It already carries the entitlement to run Cowork-built apps. Check what is deployed before assuming a greenfield consumption model.
  • The choice is a starting point, not a boundary. Start with whichever experience and commercial model fits, then combine apps, agents, and workflows as the requirement develops. Treating it as a permanent architectural fork creates a decision that does not need making.

8. The developer path

Managed apps are real code, and Microsoft publishes a first-party toolchain for teams who want to take one further or build one from scratch without the conversation. It is worth knowing this exists, because it is the answer to "what happens when our developers want to own this properly?"

  • @microsoft/managed-apps-cli on the public npm registry, providing the ms binary: create, dev, add data sources, build, pack, deploy, play, share, and delete. Companion packages supply the SDK and a Vite plugin.
  • First-party project templates and a coding-agent plugin that works with GitHub Copilot CLI and Claude Code, published in the microsoft/Managed-Apps repository.
  • GitHub Actions for CI/CD: install the CLI on the runner, pack the app, and deploy it as a service principal with no developer in the loop.
  • Least-privilege connector policies. When a connection reference is shared, the app must declare in its config which connector operations it is permitted to invoke, and the Connectors infrastructure enforces that limit per app. Tabular references declare per-table verbs; action connectors declare action IDs. Declaring everything satisfies validation and protects nothing, so infer the set from what the code actually calls.
This is a preview toolchain. The repository states plainly that APIs, templates, and tooling may change before general availability. Treat pinned versions and a rollback plan as mandatory rather than prudent.

9. When to reach for this, and when not to

A team needs an interactive tool over Microsoft 365 and business data, and needs it this weekManaged app. This is exactly the scenario, and the governance comes with it rather than after it.
A business process needs an app, an agent, and a workflow working togetherCopilot Studio, building all three side by side.
An individual or small team wants to remove a recurring manual taskManaged app from Cowork, shared by link to a named few.

10. Rollout checklist

Before you turn it on

  • Confirm Frontier enrollment is possible and identify the Global Administrator who will do it.
  • Set up usage-based billing: choose prepaid, pay-as-you-go, or both, and connect the Azure subscription if required.
  • Author the spending policy as an access decision first and a budget second. Scope it to a security group you control.
  • Audit which users already hold Power Apps Premium before modeling cost. That entitlement covers running apps built in Cowork; per app, pay-as-you-go, and Dynamics 365 licenses with Power Apps capabilities do not.
  • Decide consciously whether Auto-apply new services stays on.
  • Check your existing Power Platform environment routing configuration before enabling apps. If you already have routing rules, plan the rule covering Everyone and verify the priority order, or some makers will not be able to use apps.
  • Give finance and licensing stakeholders reader access to the cost dashboard rather than administrator roles.

In the first month

  • Watch the All apps inventory weekly while the initial wave is created. This is when ownership and duplication are cheapest to fix.
  • Set alert rules on the Monitor tab for the apps that matter, rather than waiting for users to report problems.
  • Review the Data & tools tab on any app that reaches more than a handful of users, and confirm the connectors match what you expected.
  • Check that anything heading for real production use is actually being versioned, with the published version left stable while the next one is built. An app being edited live in production is a prototype wearing a production badge.
  • Filter for apps in non-approved regions or outside expected environment groups, and act on what you find.
  • Brief builders that a share link exposes the app and its data to anyone who receives it.

Ongoing

  • Keep a deletion rhythm for abandoned, test, and duplicate apps. Prototype volume only ever grows.
  • Use Block rather than Delete for anything under investigation.
  • Export the inventory periodically for offline review and reporting.
  • Re-read the preview terms at each notable capability change. In preview, the contract moves.
